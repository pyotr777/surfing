Funboards & Mid-lengths

Mid-length boards for easy paddling, smooth glide and turns along the wave face.

What they have in common

Funboards and mid-lengths sit between shortboards and longboards. Compared with a typical shortboard, they generally have more length, planing area and float. That helps them build speed while paddling, enter a wave earlier and carry speed through its flatter sections. Being shorter than a longboard, they usually change direction more readily and draw tighter turns.

“Funboard” is often used for wide, stable boards that suit surfers building their skills. “Mid-length” is the broader name for boards of this length. The two labels overlap in shop listings and board descriptions. One such board can make it easier to learn to catch waves and turn; another can give an experienced surfer long, flowing rides and rail turns. A board in this range can be a progression step or a favourite long-term ride.

How shape changes the ride

Length helps a board glide and carry speed. A longer board will usually connect flatter parts of a wave more easily, while a shorter one is easier to put into a tighter turn. Width gives more area under the chest and feet, which normally makes paddling and balance easier. A board with a clearly pulled-in nose and tail will ride differently from one of the same length with fuller ends.

Volume in litres is a useful guide to a board’s buoyancy. Its distribution matters too. Thickness under the chest supports paddling, while thinner rails are easier to set in the water during a turn. Look at the width, thickness and whole outline as well as the litres.

Rocker is the curve from nose to tail, visible from the side. A flatter board glides readily in weak surf. More lift in the nose helps it fit a steeper drop, while tail lift helps it change direction. Rail shape also changes the feel of a turn: full, rounded rails offer one kind of support; thinner rails with a crisper edge offer another.

The bottom can be flat, gently rolled, concave or channelled. Flat and gently rolled sections help a board carry speed. Concaves and channels can add speed and responsiveness, while vee through the tail makes it easier to roll from rail to rail. A wide tail retains more area at the back; a pulled-in tail sinks more readily under pressure. These details work together, so the character of a model comes from its whole shape.

Fins complete the design. A board may have one large fin, a large centre fin with two small side fins, or two, three or four fins. Their size, flex and position change how securely the board holds in a turn and how freely it changes direction. Construction affects weight and flex too, so compare the material and the specific version as well as the shape.

Reference models

These five production models show different ways of combining paddling, glide and turns. One size is shown for each in the order “length × width × thickness”; other sizes are available in each range.

  • NSP Funboard Elements — a wide board with a semi-rounded nose, generous forward volume and a pulled-in round tail. It is easy to paddle and to learn turns on after the first rides along the face. Its bottom runs from concave in front to vee through the tail. It can be set up with three or four fins. NSP calls the Funboard one of the most popular shapes in its range.
    Example size: 7′2″ × 21½″ × 2¾″; 49 L. The Elements version has an EPS core, fibreglass skin and a stringer.

  • Channel Islands CI Mid — a Channel Islands and Devon Howard collaboration for smooth glide and long rail turns. It has an oval outline, modest nose rocker and volume set a little forward. Bottom concaves and crisp tail edges help control the board at speed. The maker particularly highlights long, shaped waves from shoulder high upward.
    Example size: 7′2″ × 21¼″ × 2¹³⁄₁₆″; 47.3 L. One large centre fin and two small side fins; the listed version uses FCS II side boxes. Two layers of fibreglass on the deck, one on the bottom.

  • Christenson C-Bucket — an elongated board with a pulled-in tail for even glide and flowing arcs. Its outline develops 1970s shapes. Low rocker, thin rails and a complex bottom let it combine glide with turning. The longer sizes lean further toward long rides along the face.
    Example size: 7′0″ × 21″ × 2¾″; 45.88 L. The standard version is a single fin, though other builds are available. Japanese model description.

  • Harley Ingleby / Tolhurst MID 6 — a Harley Ingleby and Billy Tolhurst design with an emphasis on active turns. It has a curved outline, a thin round tail, a slight nose lift and six bottom channels. The maker describes both turns near the breaking section and long arcs across the open face. In this size it is noticeably narrower than a wide funboard.
    Example size: 7′0″ × 20½″ × 2⅝″; 42 L. This reference is the Thunderbolt Red version. Five FCS boxes allow a three- or four-fin setup.

  • Firewire Seaside & Beyond — Rob Machado’s longer take on the fish idea, inspired by Skip Frye’s boards. Machado arrived at it through experiments with longer Go Fish and Seaside shapes. The model is designed to keep an easy glide and speed down the line while still turning from the tail. Firewire particularly highlights weak to moderate surf.
    Example size: 7′0″ × 21⅜″ × 2¹¹⁄₁₆″; 45.3 L. The chosen version has a Helium core and Volcanic basalt-fibre outer reinforcement.

Same length, different ride

The MID 6, C-Bucket and Seaside & Beyond above are all 7′0″ long. Their widths are 20½″, 21″ and 21⅜″, and their volumes 42, 45.88 and 45.3 L. The MID 6 stands out for its channels and thin tail for active turns; the C-Bucket for its drawn-out line and smooth glide; the Seaside & Beyond for a shape descended from the fish. When comparing boards, picture where you will stand and the line you want to draw on a wave.

After a longboard and after a shortboard

Coming from a longboard, a mid-length feels more compact. It changes direction more easily, but asks for a more precise take-off spot and a more active paddle before entering the wave. A longer, wider option can retain a familiar sense of support while you get used to new turns. Step down in size gradually, according to how confidently you already catch waves and ride along the face.

Coming from a shortboard, extra length usually makes paddling easier and helps you catch a wave earlier. The board carries speed longer, leaving more time to choose a line and draw a broad arc. For a sharper turn, move your weight closer to the tail. When choosing a size, consider your weight, experience, usual style of surfing and the waves you ride most often.

Choosing a board for the wave

On a small, gentle wave, length, area and the ability to carry speed are valuable. A wide board with gentle rocker helps you start early and cross a section where the wave has almost stopped pushing. A long, even wall is a good place for a flowing line and turns made by rolling from one rail to the other.

On a steeper, faster wave, it matters more how confidently the board enters the drop and holds its chosen line. Look at the nose rocker, rail shape and pulled-in tail. When a wave breaks quickly across its whole width, there is little time to turn even if it is not very high. Before choosing a board, consider how long the wall stays open and how much room it gives you to ride.

On Chiba waves

At Sakuta, waves are often mellow and full. On these days, a wider funboard or a longer mid-length can help you build speed early and carry it. The sandbars move, so choose a section with a wall long enough to ride before paddling out. Near the groyne, one zone is reserved for shortboards and funboards; check the boundaries and line-up order locally.

At Kanpomae, shallow banks often produce mellow surf when it is small. A funboard helps you catch waves early and carry speed across the quieter sections. Once the surf rises above head high, peaks spread out and close out more often. A funboard still works when a long, open wall remains; when the wave closes out across its width, an earlier entry no longer gives an advantage.

At nearby Shingosita, stronger surf can bring faster, steeper sections. Here the choice shifts toward a board you can direct confidently through the drop and turn. The same beach calls for a different approach on a calm day and in stronger surf: compare the shape of the wave with your experience and the way your familiar board behaves.
