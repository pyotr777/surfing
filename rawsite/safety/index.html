Safety

Safety starts on shore: check the forecast, assess the waves and currents, and decide whether the conditions suit your experience and fitness level.

In Japan, if a surfer is being swept out to sea or cannot make it back to shore without help, call the Japan Coast Guard on 118. For an ambulance, call 119.

Before you enter the water

  • Check the weather forecast for sudden changes in conditions, weather warning and hazards.
    • Weather warning / advisory : Kanto Region
  • Avoid surfing after heavy rain: runoff can pollute coastal waters. See Arnold et al. 2017. Also check the local seasonal water quality reports:
    • Chiba, 2026
    • Kanagawa, 2026
    • Ministry of the Environment
  • Watch the waves from shore. Look at their size, the time between sets, the direction they are coming from and where they break. Find the safest route to paddle out to the lineup.
  • Respect local rules, listen to experienced surfers, and pay attention to signs and warnings. Ask local surfers what you need to know about the spot.
  • Stay away from the sea if you are under the influence of alcohol or other drugs.

In the water

  • In shallow water, keep your board out of the space between you and an oncoming wave, especially with the board side-on to the wave. A wave can throw the board into you and cause a serious injury.
  • Give other surfers and their boards plenty of space. When paddling out, avoid following closely behind another surfer.
  • Always wear a leash — the cord that connects you to your board.
  • Do not let go of your board when there are people behind you.
  • A rip current is a flow of water moving away from shore that can quickly carry you out beyond the surf zone. If you are caught in one, stay with your board, do not try to paddle directly against the current, and signal for help. If you have enough strength and conditions allow, paddle parallel to the shore to get out of the current, then head back to shore.

Hazardous marine life

  • Stingrays, jellyfish, sea urchins and stonefish are among the marine animals in Japan that can cause injury. Sea urchins and stonefish are uncommon on the sandy beaches of Chiba and Kanagawa, but jellyfish and stingrays can be abundant at certain times of year. Stingrays are often found in shallow water and near river mouths from May to August. Jellyfish are most common in August and September, though some species are present almost year-round. Strong southerly winds can wash Portuguese man-of-war ashore; even dead ones can sting if touched.
  • Cover your arms and legs with a wetsuit or suitable clothing to reduce contact with jellyfish. Some sunscreens formulated to help prevent jellyfish stings may offer additional protection, but they do not guarantee that you will not be stung.
  • Stingrays rest in the shallows along sandy beaches and can be hard to spot in murky water. When wading in sandy shallows, shuffle your feet in short steps, gently stirring the sand. This helps alert stingrays to your approach and reduces the risk of stepping on one and being struck by its venomous tail spine.
  • Encounters with sharks that pose a danger to people are extremely rare off Chiba and Kanagawa. Even so, stay alert: keep away from large schools of fish and watch the water around you. The following websites carry local reports about hazardous marine life:
