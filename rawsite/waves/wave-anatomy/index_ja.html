What makes a wave

A forecast gives you numbers. These are the five that matter, and what each one does on the way to the beach.

A surf forecast is a small table of numbers, and most of the disappointment in surfing comes from reading only the first one. Height is the number everyone looks at. It is also the least informative of the five.

1. Height and period

Height says how much water is stacked up. Period — the seconds between two crests — says how that water is arranged, and it turns out to matter more. In deep water the wavelength follows directly from the period: L = gT²/2π. A six-second wind chop is about sixty metres from crest to crest; a fourteen- second groundswell is over three hundred.

That length is not a cosmetic difference. A wave stirs the water beneath it down to roughly half its wavelength, so the long swell is moving a column of water thirty metres deep while the short one barely reaches fifteen. When they arrive at the same sandbar, the long one has far more energy to convert into a breaking wave, and it does so more abruptly. Two swells reported at the same height can be different sports.

2. The angle to the coast

A swell almost never arrives square to the beach, and what happens next is refraction. As the wave moves into shallower water it slows down; the part of the crest that reaches shallow water first slows first, and the crest pivots. Over an even bottom the wave keeps turning until it is nearly parallel with the shore.

Longer-period swell refracts more, which is why a fourteen-second swell can wrap around a headland into a bay that a short-period one never enters. It is also why the swell window on each spot page is a wedge rather than a single arrow: the coast collects a range of directions and bends them into something usable.

Kugenuma, on the left, sits inside Sagami Bay and faces south — a narrow window, mostly filled by swell that has already wrapped in. Kisakihama, on the right, is exposed on an open Pacific shoulder and takes anything from northeast to south. The difference between those two wedges is the difference between waiting for a specific direction and surfing most weekends.

3. The bottom

The bottom decides the shape of the break. A wave breaks when the water beneath it becomes too shallow to carry it — roughly when the depth falls to about 1.3 times the wave height — and how fast that shallowing happens is what makes a wave spilling or plunging.

A gently sloping sandy bottom bleeds the energy out gradually and the wave crumbles from the top: the mellow, forgiving break at Kugenuma. A bottom that shallows abruptly gives the wave no time, the crest is thrown forward, and the result is the barrel at Habushiura. A rivermouth like Shioya is a third case: the bar is built by sediment and rebuilt after every flood, so the same spot can be either of the first two depending on the year.

On a much larger scale this is also why Japan's two coasts differ so completely. The Pacific side sits beside a trench thousands of metres deep, so ocean swell arrives with its energy intact. The Sea of Japan is a shallow closed basin where nothing has room to organise.

4. The wind

Wind is the only one of the five that changes hour to hour, which makes it the one worth checking last and trusting least.

Offshore wind — blowing from the land out to sea — pushes against the face of the wave as it stands up, holds it open a fraction longer and grooms the surface smooth. Onshore wind does the reverse: it makes the wave break early, all along its length at once, and leaves a chop that turns the whole lineup into moving lumps. The same swell, on the same sandbar, on the same tide, is a good day or a wasted drive depending on a wind that may not have existed when you left home.

This is why the best sessions cluster around dawn. Land cools overnight, air drains off it towards the sea, and for a couple of hours the wind is offshore almost everywhere before the daytime sea breeze sets in and ruins it.

Five numbers, in order

Read the period first — it tells you what kind of swell is coming. Then the direction, against the spot's window. Then the height, which now means something. Then the tide, because the bottom only works at certain depths. Then the wind, on the morning itself, because nothing you decided the night before survives an onshore.
