Reading a swell window

One ocean, two coastal settings, and one diagram that explains both.

A wave starts as wind pushing on water. How big it gets depends on three things: how hard the wind blows, how long it blows for, and how much open water it blows across — the fetch. Of the three, fetch is the one that decides what kind of wave you end up surfing.

With a short fetch you get wind sea: steep, close-together, disorganised. With a long fetch the waves have room to sort themselves out. Faster, longer waves outrun slower ones, and by the time the swell arrives at a coast a thousand kilometres away it has separated into clean sets with a long gap between them. Same wind, different wave, purely because of distance.

Two open coasts, different windows

All the areas in this guide face the Pacific, but they are exposed to it differently. The open beaches around Ichinomiya take northeast-to-southeast swell directly; South Boso faces south to south-east at the foot of the peninsula, where each bay gives the same swell a slightly different shape.

Late-summer typhoons can send long-period swell from far to the south and east. Ichinomiya can receive that energy directly, while South Boso is most responsive when the direction has enough south in it. Its bays and points then decide which peaks become cleanest.

The compass rose

Every spot page on this site carries a rose like the ones below. The shaded wedge is the spot's swell window — the range of directions that can actually reach it, given the shape of the coast and whatever headlands and islands sit offshore. The arrow is the wind direction that blows offshore, holding the face of the wave up instead of crumbling it.

Ichinomiya, on the left, opens from northeast through southeast and is pointed at Pacific swell. South Boso, on the right, has a south-to-south-east window. Its bays and headlands then determine how directly the swell reaches each peak. Neither is better; they reward different conditions.

Both wedges are drawn during the build. They are not images: the page reads two columns out of csv/areas.csv, converts the compass points to degrees and prints the SVG arc. Correct a swell window in the CSV and every rose on the site is redrawn on the next build.
