Reading a swell window

Two coasts, two completely different kinds of wave, and one diagram that explains both.

A wave starts as wind pushing on water. How big it gets depends on three things: how hard the wind blows, how long it blows for, and how much open water it blows across — the fetch. Of the three, fetch is the one that decides what kind of wave you end up surfing.

With a short fetch you get wind sea: steep, close-together, disorganised. With a long fetch the waves have room to sort themselves out. Faster, longer waves outrun slower ones, and by the time the swell arrives at a coast a thousand kilometres away it has separated into clean sets with a long gap between them. Same wind, different wave, purely because of distance.

Two coasts

This is the whole difference between Japan's two coastlines. The Pacific side faces open ocean and a trench that drops to thousands of metres, so a typhoon spinning north from the Philippines can generate swell two thousand kilometres away and send it here fully organised. That is why the season on the Pacific side is August to October, and why the water is 24–27 °C when the best waves arrive.

The Sea of Japan is a closed, shallow basin a few hundred kilometres across. Nothing has room to organise. What it gets instead is the winter monsoon: cold Siberian air blowing hard across the whole basin, every day, from December to March. The waves are short-period and steep, and the water is 8–11 °C. It works, but it is a different sport.

The compass rose

Every spot page on this site carries a rose like the ones below. The shaded wedge is the spot's swell window — the range of directions that can actually reach it, given the shape of the coast and whatever headlands and islands sit offshore. The arrow is the wind direction that blows offshore, holding the face of the wave up instead of crumbling it.

Tsurigasaki, on the left, opens to the northeast through southeast: it is pointed straight at where Pacific typhoon swell comes from, and a westerly off the land grooms it. Ikarashi, on the right, opens west through north — into the Sea of Japan, where the winter monsoon lives. Neither window is better. They just belong to different months.

Both wedges are drawn during the build. They are not images: the page reads two columns out of csv/spots.csv, converts the compass points to degrees and prints the SVG arc. Correct a swell window in the CSV and every rose on the site is redrawn on the next build.
